# Every loop part exists; the pairing does not

The repo's check-and-repair loop is **counterexample-guided inductive synthesis (CEGIS)** with an LLM as the learner and a sampling-based, incomplete equivalence tester as the verifier. Each part of it has strong prior work. CEGIS theory and a CEGIS loop that synthesizes programs imitating a neural policy both exist (Jha and Seshia, SYNT 2014; Zhu et al., PLDI 2019). LLMs have been placed inside CEGIS and verifier loops (Li, Parsert and Polgreen, CAV 2024; Lemur, ICLR 2024). Self-Debugging and Qiu et al. measured what counterexample feedback is worth. Angluin-style L* already extracts automata from RNNs with a net-backed equivalence oracle (Weiss et al., ICML 2018). Cryptanalytic extraction finds ReLU activation boundaries by line search (Carlini et al., CRYPTO 2020). Interpretability agents probe a black box and revise their descriptions (FIND, MAIA). The searches behind this report found **no work in which an LLM agent repairs a readable program against counterexamples from a trained feed-forward ReLU net until the two are functionally equivalent**. The combination is defensible as "not found in prior work we searched". The individual components are not novel. The repo's own logs show something the literature does not prepare a reader for. The loop never fired on the 29 formula-task nets. It fired on 7 of 12 Iris runs, and one of those took 16 checks. So the loop's value depends on the target network, not only on the agent. Step 4 should therefore measure the loop on nets where it fires, against a budget-matched no-loop baseline, with all net queries counted and every PASS confirmed by an exact verifier.

## The repo runs CEGIS with an LLM learner and a sampling oracle

In CEGIS, a learner proposes a candidate, and a verifier either accepts it or returns a counterexample that joins the learner's evidence. Jha and Seshia formalized this loop and proved that it **terminates with a correct program when the candidate space is finite and the verifier is sound**. They also showed that restricting the verifier to *minimal* counterexamples ("MinCEGIS") does not change what can be synthesized ([arXiv 1407.5397](https://arxiv.org/abs/1407.5397)). The loop is usually credited to Solar-Lezama's sketching work (ASPLOS 2006, thesis 2008); that attribution was not re-checked here. Neither of the guarantee's conditions holds in the repo. The learner is an LLM writing open-ended Python, and `check.py` tests about 200k points rather than proving equivalence. A PASS is therefore strong evidence, not a proof.

The closest published loop with a neural network as the reference behaviour is **Zhu, Xiong, Magill and Jagannathan (PLDI 2019, Distinguished Paper)**. Their method synthesizes a deterministic program that imitates a trained RL policy, together with an inductive invariant, using "counterexample and syntax-guided inductive synthesis". Each counterexample initial state triggers another program piece ([arXiv 1907.07273](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.07273); [PLDI PDF](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/suresh/papers/pldi19.pdf)). Its goal is safety preservation, not equality with the net, and its synthesizer is enumerative rather than an LLM. **VIPER** (Bastani, Pu, Solar-Lezama, NeurIPS 2018) distills a policy into a decision tree by DAgger-style imitation, then verifies the tree afterwards. Verification found a Pong counterexample, which was fixed by changing the environment rather than the tree ([NeurIPS 2018](https://proceedings.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2018/file/e6d8545daa42d5ced125a4bf747b3688-Paper.pdf)). **COMET** (NeurIPS 2020) runs SMT counterexamples against arbitrary ReLU nets, but for monotonicity rather than extraction ([NeurIPS](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2020/hash/8ab70731b1553f17c11a3bbc87e0b605-Abstract.html)).

The verifier side matters because an exact oracle exists for this setting. A ReLU MLP is piecewise linear, and so is a program built from comparisons, `max`, `min` and `abs`. "Does any input make them differ by more than ε, or disagree on argmax?" can therefore be posed as one mixed-integer linear program. **MILPEQUIV** (Kleine Büning, Kern, Sinz, CP 2020) encodes net-versus-net equivalence this way ([Kern publications](https://formal.kastel.kit.edu/~kern)). Geometric path enumeration beats it in 4 of 5 ε-equivalence benchmarks and at finding counterexamples ([arXiv 2112.06582](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2112.06582)). Carlini et al. prove that exact (ε,0)-equivalence checking is **NP-hard** in general ([ar5iv 2003.04884](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2003.04884)). With 1 to 4 inputs and a few dozen neurons, the repo's nets are far from that worst case. One more caution: a 2024 benchmark built nets with hidden counterexamples precisely because NN verifiers can be unsound ([arXiv 2412.03154](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.03154v1)). Any exact oracle added to the repo should be cross-checked against the sampler.

## Execution feedback drives LLM repair, and it saturates within three rounds

The LLM self-repair literature converges on three findings, all relevant to how `check.py` is used. First, **external execution feedback carries the gain, and self-critique alone does not**. Self-Debugging's ablation is the cleanest. On TransCoder with GPT-4, accuracy goes from a 77.3 baseline to 80.9 with a bare correct/incorrect signal, 88.8 with unit-test results, and 90.4 when an explanation is added. Without execution, the simple self-check on MBPP with Codex *fell below* baseline, 57.6 against 61.4 ([ar5iv 2304.05128](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2304.05128)). Qiu et al.'s hypothesis refinement uses a feedback template of input, expected output and actual output, which is almost exactly what `check.py` prints. Going from one to three rounds raised task accuracy from 48.0 to 59.0 on ACRE, 70.0 to 85.0 on MiniSCAN, and 52.4 to 61.2 on List Functions. When the LM acted as its own interpreter, MiniSCAN collapsed to 0.0 ([arXiv 2310.08559](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.08559)). Second, **most of the gain arrives early**. Self-Debugging's successful runs "mostly end within 3 turns" ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2304.05128)). InterpAgent saw most gains in iterations 1 and 2 ([arXiv 2605.01555](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.01555v1)). The Debugging Decay Index paper found that most of 18 models "lose 60-80% of their debugging capability within just 2-3 attempts", and that restarting with a cleared history recovered several points ([arXiv 2506.18403](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.18403v2)).

Third, and most important for step 4, **repair often barely beats resampling at equal cost**. Olausson et al. found that 10 initial GPT-4 samples with one repair each reached only 1.05x the pass rate of i.i.d. sampling on APPS, and that 2 samples with 10 repairs each did *worse*, at 0.97x. Feedback quality was the bottleneck: human feedback lifted GPT-4's repair success from 33.3% to 52.6% ([arXiv 2306.09896](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.09896v5)). The repo's checker returns exact counterexamples, so its feedback is closer to the human-quality end than to self-critique. The resampling comparison still needs to be run before claiming the loop adds value. Inside formal synthesis, the gain is clearer. In Li, Parsert and Polgreen's SyGuS experiments, GPT-3.5 re-prompted without counterexamples solved 303 of 609 benchmarks. The iLLM-synth configuration, which shows the LLM its failed candidate and the verifier's counterexample, solved 408, close to cvc5's 415. The two arms also differ in the enumerator they use, so this is not a clean ablation ([arXiv 2403.03997](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.03997v2)). Lemur's LLM-plus-verifier loop solved 25 of 47 hard SV-COMP programs, against 1 each for the standalone verifiers, averaging 7.2 proposals ([arXiv 2310.04870](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.04870)).

The literature predicts that a run still failing after three rounds rarely recovers in the same conversation. A replay of that run with a forced restart after the third FAIL would test whether the repo's agent behaves the same way.

## Net-backed oracles predate LLMs by three decades

Using a trained network as an oracle for an interpretable surrogate is old. **TREPAN** (Craven and Shavlik, NIPS 1995) grows a decision tree by querying the net for extra samples near undecided nodes. It reports fidelity and tree size, but it has no counterexample loop ([NeurIPS proceedings](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/1995/hash/45f31d16b1058d586fc3be7207b58053-Abstract.html)). The full counterexample structure appears in **Weiss, Goldberg and Yahav (ICML 2018)**. L* proposes a DFA, the trained RNN answers membership queries, and equivalence queries compare the DFA with a lazily refined abstraction of the RNN's state space. Their result also gives the repo's most direct evidence that **counterexample source matters**. On balanced parentheses, abstraction-guided counterexamples reached 99.98% agreement, against 87.12% (GRU) and 94.19% (LSTM) when random sampling served as the equivalence oracle. The extracted automata captured nesting depth 8 instead of 2 to 3 ([ar5iv 1711.09576](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1711.09576)). Mayr and Yovine replaced the equivalence query with PAC-bounded random sampling ([IFIP DL](https://dl.ifip.org/IFIP-LNCS-11015/hal-02060043)). Muškardin et al. found that model-guided conformance testing catches generalization errors that other equivalence strategies miss ([TU Graz](https://graz.elsevierpure.com/de/publications/learning-finite-state-models-fromrecurrent-neural-networks)). Their AALpy library is the one pip-installable tool in this lineage and ships 13 equivalence oracles ([PyPI](https://www.pypi.org/project/aalpy)).

For feed-forward ReLU nets, the query-based work is model stealing, and its geometry is the repo's. **Rolnick and Kording (ICML 2020)** find boundary points by querying along line segments, then assign boundary pieces to individual neurons ([PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v119/rolnick20a.html)). **Carlini, Jagielski and Mironov (CRYPTO 2020)** binary-search random lines for "critical points" where the gradient changes. They can also compute a crossing analytically by intersecting two linear pieces. With this, they recovered a 100,000-parameter MNIST net with about 2^21.5 queries, to a worst-case error of about 2^-25 ([arXiv 2003.04884](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.04884)). Hard-label extraction from top-label outputs alone now runs in polynomial time on a CIFAR-10 net with 832 neurons ([ePrint 2024/1580](https://eprint.iacr.org/2024/1580)), though follow-ups dispute how robust that is ([ePrint 2025/1868](https://eprint.iacr.org/2025/1868)). Jagielski et al. supply the vocabulary step 4 should use. *Accuracy* is task performance, while *fidelity* is agreement with the victim net, and functional equivalence is the limit case of fidelity. They argue that learning-based extraction cannot reach it ([arXiv 1909.01838](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.01838)).

## Interpretability agents probe and revise, but rarely receive counterexamples

The interpretability agents test hypotheses through the agent's own experiments, not through an external checker. **FIND** (NeurIPS 2023 Datasets and Benchmarks) gives an LLM agent black-box access to procedurally built functions and asks for a code or language description. GPT-4 recovered numeric code, meaning normalized MSE under 0.1, for only **0.33** of functions. Its signature failure is missing corruptions confined to a small part of the input domain ([arXiv 2309.03886](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.03886); [DeepAI listing](https://deepai.org/publication/a-function-interpretation-benchmark-for-evaluating-interpretability-methods)). An agent that chooses its own probes tends not to look where it has no hypothesis. A checker that sweeps the whole box with boundary points is built to catch exactly that. FIND's three arms (exemplars only, agent queries only, and both) found the combination best or tied. **MAIA** extends the setup to vision neurons with input-synthesis tools. Its tool ablation likewise found exemplars alone and generated inputs alone each worse than both together. The authors also note that its conclusions "aren't formal verification" ([arXiv 2404.14394](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.14394)).

Counterexample-driven revision appears only in passing. OpenAI's neuron explainer reports that scores rise when GPT-4 proposes counterexamples and revises "in light of their activations". The size of that effect was not retrievable ([paper page](https://openaipublic.blob.core.windows.net/neuron-explainer/paper/index.html)). Huang et al. show that one-shot GPT-4 neuron explanations have "high error rates and little to no causal efficacy" under observational and interventional tests ([arXiv 2309.10312](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.10312)). InterpAgent revises feature hypotheses against agent-generated positive prompts and matched controls. It stops when no new counterexamples appear and beats the baseline label on 63.5% to 83.3% of features, depending on the model ([arXiv 2605.01555](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.01555v1)). **LLM-SR** (ICLR 2025) is the clearest program-synthesis analogue. The LLM writes equation skeletons, an optimizer fits the constants, and an MSE score drives the next round. Removing iterative refinement raised NMSE from 2.12e-7 to 1.01e-1 on one task, though that ablation label needs checking against the paper ([arXiv 2404.18400](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.18400)). MIPS converts trained RNNs to Python and solved 32 of 62 tasks, but it has no counterexample loop ([arXiv 2402.05110](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.05110)). Across this lineage, explanations are scored by statistical proxies: correlation, F1, normalized MSE, or human preference. None gives the agent exact failing inputs against a functional-equivalence target, which is what the repo does.

## The repo's logs show the loop mattering only where data was sparse

The repo's `check.py` logs split cleanly. On **29 formula-task nets** (absolute difference, median, tax brackets, xor and others), no agent run ever received a FAIL, and no run used more than 3 checks. On **12 Iris nets**, 7 runs received at least one FAIL, 5 of them on the very first check, and iris_petal seed 2 needed 16 checks. The repairs were the "output mismatch" kind that dominated Self-Debugging's fixes: moving a threshold, or splitting one line into two half-planes joined by `or`. The simplest explanation is about where each net's behaviour comes from. The formula nets were trained on samples covering the whole input box, so their boundaries sit where the intended formula puts them, and a clean reading of the dump passes. Iris nets were trained on 150 points. Most of the box is empty, so the nets' boundaries there are arbitrary extrapolations, which uniform, grid and corner samples then probe. A program that is right on the data fails off it, and repair means copying the net's extrapolation, not recovering intent. This is FIND's localized-corruption failure in another form, and Jagielski's fidelity-versus-accuracy split applied to readable programs. Two of the seven failing Iris runs passed their first check and failed later, plausibly when a simplification broke a passing program. That is an inference that the logs can confirm.

Two caveats limit what the logs can show. First, they count only `check.py` calls. The per-neuron dump is itself executable Python, so every agent has a free membership oracle and can test its own program against it. "No FAIL on formula tasks" may mean the agent's own sampling scripts caught errors before `check.py` saw them, not that no feedback was needed. Second, 5 initial FAILs out of 12 is too small a sample to estimate repair rates. The deterministic AST tool's batch repair, which centres each threshold in the range allowed by the net's confident points, is a mechanized version of the same move. It resembles LLM-SR's split between an LLM-written skeleton and fitted constants, and the max-margin choice of a version-space learner. It can be ablated directly.

## Step 4 should measure the loop against resampling, on nets where it fires

The literature fixes the design. Olausson et al. require a **budget-matched i.i.d. baseline**, and both Self-Debugging and Qiu et al. show that **feedback content** is the variable that matters. Weiss et al. motivate a **counterexample-source** arm. FIND shows that self-directed probing differs from checker-supplied evidence. For the repo, that becomes six concrete changes.

First, **instrument every net evaluation, not just `check.py`**. Wrap the dump's forward function, or the Bash tool, so that the agent's own sampling scripts are logged with input counts. Report tokens and wall time per run. Without this, a "no checker" arm cannot be interpreted, because the agent always holds the dump as an oracle.

Second, run a **feedback-content factorial on the Iris nets**, adding more seeds or other small real-data tabular nets so that at least 30 runs start with a wrong first program. The arms are: F0, read the dump with no code execution allowed; F1, execution allowed but no `check.py` (FIND-style self-probing); F2, `check.py` returning PASS/FAIL and a mismatch count only; F3, the current counterexamples (input, net output, program output); F4, F3 with exact counterexamples from a MILP equivalence query. Report the fraction passing a strict final check as a curve over number of checks and over tokens. This is the ablation the interpretability notes found nobody had run.

Third, add a **counterexample-source sweep** inside F3. Compare uniform-only, boundary-only and the current mix, plus a k sweep of 1, 5 and all failing inputs per FAIL. This mirrors Weiss et al.'s random-versus-structured equivalence queries, and no study was found for MLPs.

Fourth, run **budget-matched resampling**. Compare n independent one-shot programs, each checked only at the end (pass@n), against one repair loop at the same token budget, and vary initial drafts and repairs per draft separately, following Olausson. Add a forced fresh restart after the third FAIL, following the Debugging Decay Index, and replay iris_petal seed 2 under it.

Fifth, **separate the loop from the LLM and from the dump**. Give the non-LLM baselines the same CEGIS wrapper: append each counterexample to PySR's or the decision tree's training set and refit until PASS, the TREPAN move made iterative. Exact region decomposition needs no loop, since it is equivalent by construction. Run the input/output-only Claude baseline as a 2×2 of {dump, no dump} × {counterexamples, PASS/FAIL only}, so that "reading weights helps" and "counterexamples help" are not confounded.

Sixth, **certify and vary the domain**. Re-verify every PASS program with an exact MILP or SMT query on the input box to measure the sampler's false-PASS rate. Rerun the Iris arms with the check restricted to the training data's bounding box, or its convex hull, and record how much repair disappears and how much more readable the programs become. For the AST tool, report held-out results with and without its batch threshold-centring step. On the formula tasks, a single cheap F0 and F1 run per net is enough to confirm that the loop is irrelevant there, which keeps the headline results honest.

## Conclusion

The novelty claim should be narrow and specific: an off-the-shelf LLM agent repairing a readable program against counterexamples from a trained feed-forward ReLU net until it is functionally equivalent, with activation-boundary counterexamples, was not found in prior work. Zhu et al. (PLDI 2019) is the precedent for the loop shape, Li et al. (CAV 2024) for an LLM inside CEGIS, Weiss et al. (ICML 2018) for a net-backed equivalence oracle, and FIND for an agent describing a function in code. The searches covered four lineages but cannot exclude unpublished hobbyist work. What is more novel than the loop is what the logs suggest about it. Counterexample refinement in decompilation measures how much of a net's behaviour lies off its training data. Formula nets had nothing off-manifold to repair, while Iris nets had little else.

That turns an apparent weakness into a research question. If repair on Iris mostly copies arbitrary extrapolation, then a fully faithful program and an intended program diverge, and step 4 should report both: fidelity over the whole box, and fidelity over the data region. The exact MILP verifier is cheap at this scale. It makes the repo's loop a sound CEGIS instance and lets it report false PASSes instead of assuming there are none. With those two additions, the repo could make a claim the cited literature does not make: how much of decompilation is reading the net, how much is the counterexamples, and how much is guessing what the net does where it was never trained.
